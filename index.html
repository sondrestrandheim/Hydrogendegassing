Hydrogen in Aluminium

About hydrogen in aluminium

Hydrogen is the only gas that has meaningful or significant solubility in aluminium and its alloys. The solubility is much higher in liquid aluminium than in solid aluminium. If the hydrogen content is too high in the melt, porosity will form during solidification.

Typical requirements

For DC-cast products: < 0.15–0.20 ml H₂/100g Al


Hydrogen source

The main source of hydrogen is moisture in the surrounding air or in refractory material. Water vapour reacts with liquid aluminium:

3H₂O(g) + 2Al(l) → Al₂O₃(s) + 3H₂(g)

Some of this hydrogen dissolves as atoms in the melt:

H₂(g) → 2[H] (dissolved in Al)

Sievert's Law

The solubility of diatomic gases in liquid metals is calculated using Sievert's Law:

S = KT · Calloy · √PH₂

where:

KT = K₀ · exp(-ΔH / RT)

Water vapour saturation in air

The amount of water vapour that can be absorbed by air is highly temperature dependent. At room temperature, the saturation is 17.3 g/m³ (~14,300 ppm water, ~1,600 ppm H₂). Higher temperature = more moisture = more hydrogen available to react with the melt.

Pressure effect on solubility

According to Sievert's Law, solubility is proportional to √PH₂. Lower pressure (e.g. at higher altitude or vacuum treatment) gives lower solubility, which drives hydrogen out of the melt.






Alloy effect on solubility

Alloying elements affect hydrogen solubility through the correction factor Calloy (Sigworth [3]):

Calloy = 10−Σei·wt%i − Σri·wt%i²

Positive e → decreases solubility (C < 1). Negative e → increases solubility (C > 1).

Argon degassing simulation

This simulation shows how argon bubbles are injected at the bottom of the melt, rise upward, and absorb hydrogen along the way. The bubbles grow due to lower hydrostatic pressure and uptake of H₂. Hydrogen is transported from the melt into the bubbles driven by the difference between equilibrium partial pressure and actual partial pressure in the bubble.

Pressure in the system

Surface pressure (atmospheric or vacuum):

Psurface = Patm or lower (with ejector/vacuum)

Normal: 1013 mbar (1 atm). With ejector: ~400–600 mbar. Strong vacuum: <200 mbar.

Metallostatic pressure (from liquid Al above the bubble):

Pmet(h) = ρAl · g · h

Total pressure at depth h:

Ptotal(h) = Psurface + ρAl · g · h

ρAl = 2350 kg/m³, g = 9.81 m/s²

Example: At 0.8 m depth, 1 atm: Pbottom = 101 325 + 18 445 = 119 770 Pa

Example with ejector (500 mbar): Pbottom = 50 000 + 18 445 = 68 445 Pa

Bubble expansion & mass transport

Ideal gas law → bubble volume expands:

V(h) = V₀ · Pbunn / P(h)
d(h) = d₀ · (Pbunn / P(h))1/3

With vacuum, Psurface is much lower → larger Pbunn/Psurface ratio → bubbles expand more → more surface area for H₂ transfer.

Driving force for H₂ transport into bubble:

ΔC = [H]melt − KT·√PH₂,bubble

Fresh argon bubble: PH₂ ≈ 0 → large driving force.
As the bubble absorbs H₂, PH₂ in the bubble increases and the driving force decreases.

Vacuum effect on driving force:

Lower Psurface → lower equilibrium [H] (Sievert: S ∝ √P). This increases ΔC, making degassing faster. This is the dominant effect of vacuum treatment.

Rise velocity (Mendelson):

v = √(2σ/(ρ·d) + g·d/2)

σ = 0.87 N/m (surface tension of liquid Al)

Why smaller bubbles = better degassing

For a given total gas volume, splitting it into smaller bubbles dramatically increases the total surface area available for hydrogen mass transfer.

Example: Consider a fixed volume V of argon gas:

  • One bubble of diameter d has surface area = πd²
  • Splitting into n smaller bubbles of diameter d/n1/3 gives total surface area = n1/3 × πd²
Atotal = n × π(d/n1/3)² = n1/3 × πd²

So splitting 1 bubble into 1000 bubbles gives 10× more surface area for the same gas volume. Additionally, smaller bubbles rise slower (longer contact time) and have a higher surface-to-volume ratio, both improving hydrogen removal efficiency.

Rotor speed and bubble size: In practice, the rotor RPM is the main way to control bubble size. A faster spinning rotor shears the argon into finer bubbles through higher tip speed and turbulent breakup. Typical relationships:

  • Low RPM (~200–400): Large bubbles, 10–20 mm — poor degassing
  • Medium RPM (~500–700): 3–8 mm — good for most applications
  • High RPM (~800–1200): 1–3 mm — excellent degassing, but higher dross generation and energy use

In the simulation, adjust the bubble start diameter to represent the effect of different rotor speeds.

Mass transfer coefficient kL

The rate of hydrogen transfer from melt to bubble depends on the mass transfer coefficient kL, calculated using Higbie penetration theory (1935):

kL = 2 · √(DH,Al / (π · tc))

where:

  • DH,Al = diffusivity of hydrogen in liquid Al (m²/s)
  • tc = contact time = d / v (bubble diameter / rise velocity)

The idea: a fresh "element" of melt contacts the bubble surface. During the short contact time tc, hydrogen diffuses into the bubble. A smaller bubble has shorter contact time but there are many more of them with much more total surface area — the net effect is that smaller bubbles give faster degassing.

Key insight: kL ∝ √(D · v / d), so both diffusivity and bubble size matter.

Diffusivity DH,Al(T)

The diffusion coefficient of hydrogen in liquid aluminium follows an Arrhenius relationship (Eichenauer et al.):

DH,Al(T) = D₀ · exp(−Ea / RT)
